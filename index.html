Section 1: Cerebral Edema & Protection of Brain Perfusion

Severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) can produce secondary cerebral edema, impaired autoregulation and intracranial hypertension. An elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) reduces the pressure gradient available to perfuse the brain. Preventing hypoxia and hypotension remains essential while definitive neurocritical and neurosurgical care proceeds.

1.1 Cerebral Perfusion Pressure (CPP)

Clinical thresholds: The Brain Trauma Foundation recommends treating ICP above 22 mm Hg; a CPP goal of 60–70 mm Hg is generally recommended in severe TBI.

Clinical interpretation: MAP must be evaluated alongside measured ICP. A fixed MAP of 80–90 mm Hg is not a universal target; individualize to achieve CPP while avoiding excessive fluids and vasopressors.

1.2 Hyperosmolar Therapy: 3% Saline Versus Mannitol

3% hypertonic saline

Mechanism / pharmacodynamics

Raises serum tonicity; draws water from cerebral tissue into vascular compartment.

Hemodynamic effects

May expand intravascular volume; often favored with hypotension risk.

Monitoring / precautions

Trend serum Na+, Cl−, renal function and fluid balance. No universal mandatory sodium target of 145–155 mEq/L.

20% mannitol

Mechanism / pharmacodynamics

Osmotic agent that reduces brain water and promotes osmotic diuresis.

Hemodynamic effects

Can worsen volume depletion and hypotension, particularly in hemorrhagic shock.

Monitoring / precautions

Monitor urine output, kidney function, volume status and osmolar gap; avoid in clinically important hypovolemia.

Barrier reflection coefficient:

Hypertonic saline is often modeled with a reflection coefficient near 1.0 and mannitol near 0.9 across an intact blood–brain barrier; these are idealized values, not literal percentages of drug retained.

Treatment selection:

Use clinical context, local protocols and serial ICP/neurologic assessments. Hyperosmolar therapy treats cerebral swelling; it does not replace hemorrhage control or reversal of herniation causes.

1.3 Hemodynamic Support in Brain and Spinal Cord Injury

Norepinephrine: A commonly used vasopressor for MAP support after adequate volume assessment, with careful evaluation of cardiac output and peripheral perfusion.

Phenylephrine: Pure alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction may cause reflex bradycardia and reduce cardiac output in selected patients.

Acute traumatic spinal cord injury: The 2024 guideline suggests augmenting MAP to a lower bound of 75–80 mm Hg, without actively augmenting above an upper bound of 90–95 mm Hg, for 3–7 days; certainty is low.

Section 2: Urgent Reversal of Anticoagulants in Intracranial Hemorrhage

Anticoagulant-associated intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is a time-sensitive emergency. Immediately discontinue the anticoagulant, establish the agent and last dose, obtain appropriate coagulation studies, and coordinate urgent neurocritical care, neurosurgery, pharmacy and blood bank support. Reversal is initiated without waiting for all laboratory results when clinical indications are clear.

Section 3: Vertigo Pharmacology & Syncope Evaluation

3.1 Central Versus Peripheral Vertigo

Acute dizziness should be categorized by timing, triggers and examination findings. Posterior circulation stroke may present without obvious limb weakness. Red flags include vertical or direction-changing nystagmus, new diplopia or dysarthria, marked gait/truncal ataxia and other focal neurologic abnormalities.

The HINTS examination is appropriate only in the correct acute vestibular syndrome population and when performed by appropriately trained clinicians. A concerning or equivocal examination warrants stroke evaluation. For benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), canalith repositioning maneuvers are preferred over routine long-term suppressants.

3.3 Syncope: Medication Review and High-Risk Screen

Syncope is transient loss of consciousness from global cerebral hypoperfusion with prompt recovery. Evaluation focuses on unstable arrhythmia, structural cardiac disease, pulmonary embolism when suspected, and other immediately dangerous causes, while considering reflex and orthostatic mechanisms.

High-risk findings: Exertional syncope, abnormal ECG, known structural heart disease, chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, significant injury or persistent hemodynamic instability.
First-line workup:

History (including witness account), physical examination, vital signs, 12-lead ECG, orthostatic measurements when appropriate, and targeted rather than indiscriminate tests.

Medication triggers:

Alpha-1 blockers such as tamsulosin, antihypertensive vasodilators, diuretics, beta-blockers, nondihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers and selected antiarrhythmics.
